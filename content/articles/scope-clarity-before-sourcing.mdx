## Executive summary

A quotation is easier to compare when the need, delivery boundary, acceptance evidence, and decision owner are explicit. This developing note proposes a simple review method; it is not a universal procurement standard.

Read the comparison below, then use the [blank scope checklist](/resources/scope-review-checklist) alongside its [fictional filled example](/resources/scope-review-checklist/example). For the wider connection to supplier evidence and data, follow [From requirement to decision](/atlas/requirement-to-decision).

## Context

A request such as ‘repair the equipment and make it work properly’ leaves several commercial questions unanswered. Vendors may price different work even when their quotations share the same title.

## Core concept

Scope describes what will be delivered. Acceptance describes the evidence needed to decide whether that delivery meets the need. Both need an owner who can resolve ambiguity before award.

The [UN Procurement Practitioner's Handbook](#reference-un-pph-requirements), section 5.2, treats requirement descriptions as the basis for evaluating offers. Section 7.3 relates acceptance to the agreed requirements. This supports connecting scope and acceptance; the review below is this note's proposed working aid.

## A four-question review

Use these questions before requesting comparable offers. The level of detail should match the complexity and consequences of the work.

1. Need: what business or operational problem must be resolved?
2. Boundary: what is included, excluded, and dependent on another party?
3. Evidence: what test, inspection, or document will demonstrate acceptance?
4. Ownership: who defines the technical need, approves changes, and accepts delivery?

## Practical example

For a fictional pump repair, one offer includes bearing replacement only; another includes shaft inspection and alignment. Comparing the totals would be misleading. Agree a common scope, request separate prices for optional work, and record the acceptance test before evaluating the commercial offers.

## Compare a common promise

Use the same fictional training-booklet request as the filled checklist: 20 booklets, each with 24 A4 monochrome pages printed duplex on 12 sheets and stapled, delivered by 21 October 2026. The requester supplies a final PDF. The paper choice and proof legibility criteria still need agreement.

The two offers below are invented contrasts for this exercise. They are not actual quotations or an assessment of any supplier.

| Scope item              | Fictional offer A                               | Fictional offer B                                            |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Finished booklets       | 20 copies; page count and binding are unstated. | 20 copies; the requested page count and stapling are stated. |
| Proof before production | No proof round is mentioned.                    | One proof round is included.                                 |
| Delivery                | Collection at the printer is assumed.           | Delivery to the fictional training room is included.         |
| Optional work           | No colour cover is mentioned.                   | A colour cover is offered as an optional line.               |
| Changed PDF             | No revision treatment is stated.                | A second proof is excluded; its price is unstated.           |

**What can be concluded?** The totals would describe different promises. A is not automatically cheaper for the required delivery, and B is not automatically ready to order. Neither contrast resolves the paper, legibility, or changed-file questions.

**What should be clarified?** Ask both options to confirm the same booklet specification, one proof round, delivery destination and date, assumptions, and exclusions. Keep the colour cover separate from the monochrome requirement. Record how a changed PDF would affect proofing, price, schedule, and approval. Leave an unanswered item marked as unknown; do not assume it is included at no cost.

The learning output is a common-scope comparison with unresolved items visible. It is not a supplier ranking or evidence of savings.

## Make acceptance reviewable

An inspection activity and a passing condition answer different questions. “Check a proof” names an activity; it does not explain which proof the reviewer can accept.

For this fictional request, propose three connected records:

1. **Deliverable and criterion:** identify the approved PDF version, page order, quantity, and binding to check. Ask the requester and reviewer to agree paper and legibility criteria; this note does not supply a tested threshold.
2. **Method and conditions:** state when the proof is reviewed, what it is compared with, and which reading conditions matter. Record deviations and what requires another proof before production.
3. **Evidence and owner:** record the proof decision, its basis, the reviewer, and the approved version. Delivery counting and proof approval are distinct checks; a delivery receipt alone does not demonstrate that the print meets the need.

These prompts adapt the distinction between contract requirements and inspection in [handbook section 7.3](#reference-un-pph-requirements). The proposed records are this note's learning aid, not forms required by the handbook.

## Trade-offs

Trying to specify every uncertainty can delay sourcing without adding value. For genuinely uncertain work, define the inspection stage, pricing mechanism, approval point, and evidence for any additional scope instead of pretending the uncertainty has disappeared.

For the booklet example, a revised PDF is a possible change, not part of an unlimited promise. Record the proposed change, its effect on the deliverable and timing, the pricing basis, and the approval required before changed production. [Handbook section 7.4](#reference-un-pph-requirements) discusses documented changes and authorised ownership in the UN context. Apply the organisation's own procedure; this example does not establish contractual authority.

## Key takeaways

Before comparing prices, compare the promise being priced. Next, try the scope review checklist on one low-risk request and record which questions it helps resolve.

## A small review exercise

Choose a fictional or safely sanitized request. Have the requester and a reviewer independently describe what is included and what evidence would justify acceptance. Compare their answers, record unresolved differences, and revise the request before asking for prices. This is a proposed learning exercise, not evidence of improved commercial results.

Start with the [filled booklet example](/resources/scope-review-checklist/example), then try the blank checklist:

1. Write one clarification for offer A's delivery assumption and one for offer B's changed-file exclusion. Explain why each could change the comparison.
2. Separate a proposed acceptance check from a criterion that is still missing. Assign the unresolved question to the fictional requester or reviewer without marking it resolved.
3. Write a decision sentence stating what can be compared, what remains unknown, and the next check. Keep “ready to clarify” distinct from approval to order.

<Callout title="One possible learning answer">

The booklet quantities can be aligned, but the delivered scope and changed-file treatment cannot yet be compared on the same basis. Ask both options to state proofing and delivery inclusions and the revision price basis; ask the requester and reviewer to agree paper and legibility criteria. Keep the request in draft until those answers are reviewed. Other answers may expose additional gaps.

</Callout>

For a later practical trial, retain the request version, the two reviewers' disagreements, questions the checklist missed, and the resulting revision. A completed exercise or checklist does not close this note's evidence gaps.

<Callout title="Applying the source">

The handbook describes practices in the UN system. Adapt this aid to the organisation's own approval process and technical requirements; neither the source nor the pump example validates a repair specification.

</Callout>
